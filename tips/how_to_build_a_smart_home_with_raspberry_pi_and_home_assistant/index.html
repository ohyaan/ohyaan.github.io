    How to Build a Smart Home with Raspberry Pi and Home Assistant¶

    Smart home automation is one of the most practical and exciting projects you can build with a Raspberry Pi. While off-the-shelf commercial hubs like Apple HomeKit, Google Home, or Amazon Alexa are convenient, they often lock you into their ecosystems and send your data to the cloud.

    Home Assistant (HA) is a powerful, open-source smart home platform that prioritizes local control and privacy. Running Home Assistant on a Raspberry Pi allows you to unify devices from different manufacturers, run complex automations locally, and keep your data completely inside your home.

    This comprehensive guide will walk you through setting up Home Assistant OS (HAOS) on a Raspberry Pi, integrating commercial smart devices, wiring physical GPIO sensors, and setting up automated workflows.

    Why Raspberry Pi and Home Assistant?¶

    • Local Control: Devices respond instantly because automation logic runs on your local network—no cloud round-trips required.
    • Privacy-First: Your data (when you are home, sensor readings, camera feeds) remains stored locally on your Pi.
    • Massive Compatibility: Home Assistant supports over 2,500 integrations (SwitchBot, Philips Hue, ESPHome, Zigbee, Z-Wave, etc.).
    • GPIO Extensibility: Unlike commercial hubs, you can connect cheap electronics components (like temperature, motion, or light sensors) directly to the Pi's GPIO pins.

    Hardware Requirements¶

    To build a reliable Home Assistant hub, you will need the following hardware:

    Component Recommended Specification Why it matters
    Raspberry Pi Raspberry Pi 4 (4GB+) or Raspberry Pi 5 Provides ample CPU and RAM for running multiple add-ons and processing camera feeds.
    Storage High-End Application Class MicroSD (A2) or SSD Home Assistant writes logs and database entries constantly. Cheap SD cards will corrupt quickly. SSD is highly recommended for long-term stability.
    Power Supply Official Raspberry Pi Power Supply Prevents under-voltage warnings and database corruption.
    Sensor (Optional) DHT22 (AM2302) Temperature & Humidity Sensor For hands-on physical sensor integration.
    Breadboard & Jumpers Female-to-Female jumper wires For connecting the sensor to the GPIO pins.

    Step 1: Installing Home Assistant OS (HAOS)¶

    Home Assistant OS is a minimal, optimized operating system designed specifically to run Home Assistant. It manages containers, backups, and OS updates automatically via a clean web interface.

    1. Insert your MicroSD card or SSD into your computer.
    2. Open Raspberry Pi Imager.
    3. Click Choose OS -> Other specific-purpose OS -> Home assistants and home automation -> Home Assistant.
    4. Choose the version corresponding to your hardware model (e.g., Home Assistant OS for Raspberry Pi 4 or Pi 5).
    5. Click Choose Storage and select your target drive.
    6. Click Next and write the image.
    7. Once written, insert the storage drive into your Raspberry Pi, connect an Ethernet cable to your router, and power it on.

    [!NOTE] The initial boot and installation process can take up to 20 minutes as the Pi downloads the latest Home Assistant containers and sets up the internal database. Do not unplug the power during this phase.

    To access your new hub, open a browser on a device connected to the same local network and navigate to: 

    1
    http://homeassistant.local:8123
    Follow the on-screen prompts to create your owner account and set your location (used for sunrise/sunset automations).

    Step 2: Integrating Commercial Smart Devices¶

    One of Home Assistant's greatest strengths is its ability to automatically discover smart devices on your local network.

    1. Go to Settings -> Devices & Services.
    2. You will likely see discovered devices (e.g., Google Cast, Apple TV, Philips Hue, ESPHome, or TP-Link Smart Plugs) waiting to be configured.
    3. Click Configure on any detected device and follow the authentication steps.
    4. To add a device manually, click + Add Integration at the bottom right, search for your device brand (such as SwitchBot), and enter the configuration details.

    Reading Sensor Data in Home Assistant¶

    For stability and ease of configuration, using ESPHome (via a cheap $4 ESP8266 or ESP32 micro-controller connected to the Pi via USB or Wi-Fi) is the modern best practice.

    However, if you want to read directly from the Pi's own GPIO pins, you can use the Raspberry Pi Remote GPIO custom integration:

    1. Install the Terminal & SSH add-on from the Add-on Store (Settings -> Add-ons).
    2. Open the terminal and edit your config file: 
      1
      nano /config/configuration.yaml
    3. Add the configuration to define the DHT22 sensor on GPIO 17:

  - platform: dht

      - temperature

    4. Restart Home Assistant (Settings -> System -> Restart). You will now have two new entities: sensor.living_room_temp_humidity_temperature and sensor.living_room_temp_humidity_humidity.

    Step 4: Creating Smart Automations¶

    Automations in Home Assistant follow a simple structure: Triggers (When...), Conditions (But only if...), and Actions (Then do...).

    Let's create an automation that automatically turns on a humidifier (connected to a smart plug) when the living room humidity drops below 40%, but only if someone is home.

    UI Method¶

    1. Navigate to Settings -> Automations & Scenes -> Create Automation.
    2. Trigger: Click Add Trigger, choose Numeric State, select your humidity sensor entity, and set Below to 40.
    3. Condition: Click Add Condition, choose State, select your tracking entity (e.g., zone.home), and set the state to 1 (or check if a presence sensor is home).
    4. Action: Click Add Action, choose Device, select your Humidifier Smart Plug, and set the action to Turn On.
    5. Click Save and give it a name like "Auto Humidifier ON".

    Step 5: Secure Remote Access with Tailscale¶

    By default, Home Assistant is only accessible within your home network. To control your home while away, you need a secure remote access solution. Opening ports (port forwarding) on your router is risky. The safest approach is utilizing a Zero Trust mesh VPN like Tailscale.

    1. In Home Assistant, navigate to Settings -> Add-ons -> Add-on Store.
    2. Search for Tailscale and install it.
    3. Start the add-on, open its Web UI, and authenticate with your Tailscale account.
    4. Enable Exit Node or simply use the assigned Tailscale IP address (e.g., 100.x.y.z) to connect securely to http://100.x.y.z:8123 from your phone or laptop anywhere in the world.

    Conclusion¶

    Running Home Assistant on your Raspberry Pi shifts the control of your house back into your hands. From here, the possibilities are virtually limitless. You can add Zigbee dongles to connect low-power wireless sensors, integrate local cameras with AI-powered object detection (via Frigate), or build custom dashboards to display on tablets wall-mounted around your home.

    By avoiding cloud dependencies, your home remains fast, secure, and fully functional even if your internet connection goes down.
